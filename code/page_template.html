Britain in AD 500

What the ground tells us. Reused hillforts, Anglo-Saxon graves, Latin and ogham stones, Pictish symbols and early churches on one map, with the kingdoms we know mostly from later writers shown as what they are: names without borders.

There is a famous kind of map that shows Britain around AD 500 as a patchwork of kingdoms: Rheged and Elmet, Gwynedd and Dumnonia, Bernicia, Kent and the East Angles, each in its own colour with a neat edge. I like those maps. They are beautiful, and they make a lost world feel real. But almost nothing written in Britain survives from the century after Rome, and most of those kingdom names first appear in books written two, three or seven hundred years later.

So I tried to build a different map: one that starts from the ground. It plots what archaeology and the national records can actually place in the years around 500, from hillforts that people went back to, to cemeteries, inscribed memorial stones and early churches. Then it lays the named kingdoms on top, not as countries with borders but as faint, labelled zones, each with a note on where and when its name is first written down.

The result is patchy, and I will say where. But some patterns are so strong that even thin, uneven data shows them.

    02 · Two habitsAn east of graves, a west of stones

    Put the Anglo-Saxon cemeteries and settlements next to the Latin-inscribed memorial stones and the island splits in two. The {{eastwest.germanic_n|int}} Anglo-Saxon sites in my data have a median longitude of {{eastwest.germanic_median_lon|w2}} degrees west, almost on the Greenwich meridian; for the {{eastwest.brittonic_n|int}} inscribed stones and western high-status sites it is {{eastwest.brittonic_median_lon|w2}} degrees west, in the middle of Wales. {{eastwest.germanic_east_of_m2|pct}} of the Anglo-Saxon sites lie east of 2 degrees west, and {{eastwest.brittonic_west_of_m3|pct}} of the stones lie west of 3 degrees west. Just {{eastwest.brit_within_50km_of_ger|int}} of those western sites has an Anglo-Saxon site within 50 km. Smoothed into density surfaces, the two never overlap: there is no square kilometre where both rise above a tenth of their peak.

    Graves in the east, stones in the west

    Data: Wikidata items whose names or descriptions mark them as early Anglo-Saxon cemeteries, burials or settlements; Wikidata inscribed stones in Britain outside the Pictish north; the high-status sites in section 03. See How I did this.

    This is a real pattern, and archaeologists have described it for a long time, but it is worth being clear about what it is. It is a map of two habits, not two peoples. In the east, people buried their dead with brooches, beads and weapons, or cremated them in urns, in the way that came across the North Sea in the 5th century. In the west, Christian Britons set up memorial stones carved with Latin names, often with Roman-style titles. Somebody living in Kent who never owned a brooch, or a family in Wales too poor for a stone, is invisible on this map. Even so, the gap in the middle is striking: a band from the Pennines to Dorset where neither habit shows in my data.

    CaveatWikidata's record of Anglo-Saxon cemeteries is thin: it holds {{layers.germanic|int}} that I could identify, far fewer than archaeologists know about, and they are mostly scheduled monuments in England whose official names happen to say "Anglo-Saxon". The western stones are better covered because Cadw's scheduled monument names say "inscribed stone". So treat the counts as samples and the contrast as the finding.

    03 · Back to the hillsThe hillforts people went back to

    Most hillforts were built in the Iron Age, centuries before Rome. The Atlas of Hillforts of Britain and Ireland, compiled by Gary Lock and Ian Ralston, records {{hill.atlas_total|int}} of them, with a field for every period in which there is dating evidence. Just {{hill.dated|int}} ({{hill.share|pct1}}) have evidence for use between AD 400 and 800. They are not spread evenly: {{hill.by_country.Scotland.dated|int}} are in Scotland, {{hill.by_country.England.dated|int}} in England, {{hill.by_country.Wales.dated|int}} in Wales and {{hill.by_country.Isle of Man.dated|int}} on the Isle of Man, where that is {{hill.by_country.Isle of Man.share|pct}} of the island's {{hill.by_country.Isle of Man.n|int}} hillforts.

    Share of hillforts with dating evidence for AD 400 to 800
    By country, Atlas of Hillforts of Britain and Ireland. Bar length: share; label: number dated and total.

    The ones that were reused look different from the average hillfort. {{hill.coastal_share|pct}} are on the coast, against {{hill.coastal_all_share|pct}} of all hillforts, about {{hill.coastal_ratio|x1}} times the rate; {{hill.promontory_share|pct}} are promontory forts, against {{hill.promontory_all_share|pct}}. That fits what excavation has found at the famous sites: these were small, defended seats of chiefs and kings on headlands and crags by the sea, the places where wine and fine pottery from the Mediterranean and Gaul came ashore.

    The dating is uneven. The Atlas rates its own confidence: for {{hill.reliable_ab|int}} of the {{hill.dated|int}} the evidence is rated high or medium, and for {{hill.no_reliability|int}} it is rated as none, meaning the early medieval date rests on something like a place-name or a stray find. {{hill.radiocarbon|int}} of the dating notes mention radiocarbon dates.

    04 · Across the Irish SeaOgham, Dyfed and the western sea

    Ogham is an alphabet of strokes and notches cut along the edge of a stone, used mainly to write the early Irish language. My map has {{ogham.spots|int}} ogham find-spots, merged from {{ogham.records|int}} records in Wikidata and Ireland's Sites and Monuments Record, and {{ogham.ireland_share|pct}} of them are in Ireland. Wikipedia's article on ogham says the stones flourished in the 5th and 6th centuries around the Irish Sea, and that the largest number outside Ireland is in Pembrokeshire.

    That second point is where my data falls short, and honestly so. In Wikidata most Welsh stones are recorded only as "inscribed stones", whether or not they also carry ogham, so the ogham layer shows just {{ogham.by_nation.Wales|int}} in Wales. What the map can show is the stones themselves: {{latin.wales_share|pct}} of the {{latin.n|int}} inscribed stones are in Wales, and {{latin.dyfed|int}} of them ({{latin.dyfed_share|pct}}) are in the old county of Dyfed in the south-west. The written sources point the same way. Wikipedia's article on the kingdom of Dyfed says its royal line was founded by Irish settlers in the 5th century. A memorial stone from Castell Dwyran, of about 550, names a king in Latin and in ogham and gives him the late Roman title of protector. He is often identified with Vortipor, the king of the Demetae whom Gildas attacks by name, although the spellings differ and some scholars doubt it.

    One stone, two alphabets, and perhaps a king who appears in Gildas: that is about as close as AD 500 comes to a named person on the ground.

    The traffic went north too. Dál Riata, in Argyll, was a Gaelic kingdom that spanned the sea to Antrim, and its chief seat is thought to have been the crag of Dunadd, with its carved footprint for king-making. In Brycheiniog, the Brecon kingdom, tradition has an Irish-descended founder called Brychan, and Wikipedia's article says it was probably founded by Irish raiders in the late 5th century.

    05 · Names without bordersThe kingdoms

    Here are the {{kingdoms.n|int}} kingdoms on the map: {{kingdoms.n_brittonic|int}} British, {{kingdoms.n_germanic|int}} Anglo-Saxon, one Gaelic and one Pictish. For each I give the earliest written mention as Wikipedia's article describes it, and what evidence lies in the zone I drew. Some zones are full of evidence and some are empty. An empty zone does not mean an empty kingdom: Elmet and Ergyng, for example, are known from texts and place-names, and their people left little that my layers can see.

    Look at the right-hand column and a pattern appears: for the southern and eastern Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, almost all the culturally distinctive evidence in the zone is Anglo-Saxon, and for Wales almost all of it is British. In the north it is thinner. Gododdin's zone, around Edinburgh and Traprain Law, catches Pictish stones from across the Forth and hillforts with no cultural label, and nothing that says Gododdin. That is what most of this period looks like when you try to pin names to the ground.

    06 · What Rome leftTowns, forts and the new settlers

    Under the early medieval layers sits the Roman province. Pleiades, the gazetteer of the ancient world, places {{roman.towns|int}} Roman towns and settlements and {{roman.forts|int}} forts in my frame with good locations. It tags {{roman.late_town_share|pct}} of the towns and {{roman.late_fort_share|pct}} of the forts with its Late Antique period (AD 300 to 640). Most of that reflects the 4th century, not proof that anyone lived in them in 500. A few have stronger stories: Wikipedia's article on Wroxeter says the Roman town may have served as an early post-Roman capital of Powys.

    The Anglo-Saxon sites in my data sit close to Roman towns. Their median distance to the nearest Roman town is {{roman.ger_town_med_km|f1}} km, against {{roman.random_town_med_km|f1}} km for random points in the same part of England, and {{roman.ger_within_10km_town|pct}} lie within 10 km of one, against {{roman.random_within_10km_town|pct}} of the random points. It is a modest difference, and it has an obvious dull explanation as well as an exciting one: Roman towns and early Anglo-Saxon farms both favoured the same good river-valley land.

    07 · Dark skiesThe 530s and 540s

    Two disasters hit the middle of the century. In 536 a volcanic eruption filled the northern sky with a veil of sulphate haze and summers turned cold for years. Then the plague of Justinian spread from Egypt in 541 around the Mediterranean, and burials at Edix Hill near Cambridge show that it reached Britain. Neither can be seen in my layers, because the dating of most of the evidence is far too broad. The chart below sets the few firm dates against the evidence that is "in play" at each date.

    08 · Why so thin?Writing the 5th century

    Almost everything we know by name about Britain in 500 comes from texts written long afterwards. The chart shows the gap. Gildas, a British monk, is the only writer close to the time, and even his book is dated anywhere from the late 5th century to the 540s. Bede finished his history in 731. The Historia Brittonum, which gives us Arthur's battles and the British kings of the north, is of the early 9th century. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle survives in manuscripts compiled from the 9th to the 12th centuries. The poem Y Gododdin survives in one manuscript of the later 13th century.

    When the main sources were written
    Each bar runs from the date usually given for the text's composition to its earliest surviving copy where Wikipedia gives one. The shaded band is AD 450 to 550.

    That is why every kingdom on my map is drawn in italics with a soft edge. The names are real: they mattered to the people who wrote them down. But they reach us through later politics, genealogies built to flatter later kings, and stories that grew in the telling. The ground, by contrast, is mute but contemporary.

    How I did this

    Hillforts. The Atlas of Hillforts of Britain and Ireland (Lock and Ralston 2017), downloaded from its ArcGIS service on 28 October 2026: {{hill.atlas_total|int}} records. A hillfort is in my layer if the Atlas marks dating evidence for AD 400 to 800; the popup quotes the Atlas's own dating note and its reliability rating.

    Wikidata. I downloaded every item in the United Kingdom, Ireland and the Isle of Man whose type matches a long list of site kinds (cemeteries, barrows, inscribed stones, ogham stones, Pictish stones, churches, monasteries, forts and more), {{coverage.wd_items_frame|int}} items with coordinates in my frame, and filtered them hard. Anglo-Saxon sites: names or descriptions that say Anglo-Saxon, Anglian or early Saxon cemetery, burial or settlement, excluding later ("middle Saxon", "late Saxon", burhs, churches and crosses). Inscribed stones: Britain only, excluding cross-carved slabs, Pictish and Roman stones. Pictish stones: Scotland. Churches and monasteries: a start date between 400 and 700 in Wikidata, read as a century where Wikidata stores one, plus Irish sites named "early medieval ecclesiastical site". Ogham: Wikidata ogham stones and sites, plus the ogham stones in Ireland's Sites and Monuments Record at their original locations, merged into find-spots where records lie within 300 m of each other.

    High-status sites and dykes. A short hand-made list, each entry tied to a sentence in the Wikipedia article that I checked against the downloaded text, with coordinates from the article or Wikidata (Wansdyke: Tan Hill, Wiltshire, from OS Open Names).

    Dates. {{site_dated_total|int}} pieces of evidence have a date range of their own (the Atlas's AD 400 to 800 band, Wikidata start dates, or a date in the Wikipedia text). The other {{type_dated_total|int}} carry a typical range for their kind: Anglo-Saxon cemeteries 450 to 650, ogham stones 400 to 600, Latin stones 400 to 700, Pictish stones 500 to 850. The slider shows what overlaps each year.

    Haze. For each tradition I counted the evidence on a web-mercator grid of about 1.5 km and smoothed it with a Gaussian kernel of {{surfaces.sigma_km|int}} km, then scaled it to its own 99th percentile. Germanic: Anglo-Saxon sites and the Anglian high-status sites. Brittonic: Latin stones and British high-status sites. Irish: ogham and Dunadd. Pictish: Pictish stones. Hillforts, churches and Roman sites are not given a culture. The haze shows where evidence is dense in my data, not where people lived.

    Kingdoms. I placed each name at the area its Wikipedia article points to (a capital, a Roman town or a region) and drew a soft circle whose size is only a guide. The table's "first written mention" summarises the article, and each key phrase was checked against the downloaded text. Ireland's many kingdoms are not labelled.

    Low-lying land. Land under 5 m above sea level today, from AWS Terrain Tiles, as a rough guide to where fen and marsh lay ({{coverage.lowland_km2|int}} km²). Coasts and drainage have changed since 500, so this is a sketch.

    Known problems. Wikidata coverage differs by country and by heritage agency: Wales's scheduled stones are well named, England's Anglo-Saxon cemeteries poorly; Ireland's Sites and Monuments Record covers the Republic only; Pictish stones number about {{pictish.wikipedia_survive|int}} according to Wikipedia, and my layer has {{pictish.n|int}}. Many "inscribed stones" have been moved into churches and museums. The search uses OS Open Names for Great Britain and GeoNames for Ireland, Northern Ireland and the Isle of Man.
